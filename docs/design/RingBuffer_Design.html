RingBuffer design

How Engine/Container/RingBuffer.h gets a lock-free hot path, bounded memory and safe block recycling out of a chain of blocks, each one a pluggable fixed-size ring
Scope. This document owns the structure, the invariants, the memory ordering, the recycling protocol and the evidence behind them. What a caller may depend on — who may call what, the meaning of each return value, what destruction does — lives on the reference pages: the class page and its Push, Pop and IsEmpty.

Why this shape

The requirement was a queue carrying on the order of millions of records between many producer threads and one consumer, where a producer must never block and a stalled producer must not stall the queue. The engine already had an intrusive MPSC queue — producers pushed a node they owned, the consumer reclaimed the whole producer stack with one exchange and reversed it — and that design was the right answer to a different question. Here is where it stops answering this one.

PropertyIntrusive view queueRing-blocked queue
Who owns the element The caller; the queue links a node it was handed, which is why Push could not fail and why the destructor released nothing The queue; Push copies into its own storage and Pop destroys that copy, so the destructor owns what is still queued
Memory requests One per element at the call site — the caller's allocation, invisible to the queue, and the reason a "view" container cannot batch One per block — one per BlockCapacity elements — from the allocator that was current at construction, charged to it in the ledger
A producer that stalls mid-push Invisible. The node does not exist in the queue until the pointer lands, so the consumer cannot know a value is coming and cannot report it Visible as EPopResult::Blocked. The slot is claimed before the payload is written, so the consumer sees a commitment and can go and do something useful instead of spinning or losing the element
Reclamation Not the queue's problem, because it never owned anything The queue's problem, and the subject of most of this document — owning memory is what makes recycling, and therefore proof, necessary
Batch FIFO reversal Needed: a stack flipped into a queue has to be reversed, and the reversal is the trickiest part of that design Not needed: a ring is traversed in order, so there is nothing to reverse

Structure

A queue is a chain of blocks, and a block is one fixed-size ring supplied as the template parameter U with one claim counter in front of it. The division of labour is the whole point of the redesign: the chain owns the claim counter, the successor link, the block's memory and the decision about when a block may be recycled; the ring owns the slots, the payloads, their publication and the consumer's position inside the block. Producers work at the tail of the chain, the consumer at the head, and the chain only ever grows at the tail.

The block's two hot ends are the two that are spaced at LineSpacing, which is std::hardware_destructive_interference_size: the claim word, which producers collide on, and the ring, whose publication words and payload the consumer reads. The allocation handle and the successor pointer sit between them and are written once, so a reservation and a dequeue land on different lines.

The slot arithmetic has moved inside the backend, so the chain no longer computes a slot address at all. What the chain still requires of a backend is stated as a concept rather than assumed: CFixedRing<T, U> refuses a U whose GetCapacity() is not a power of two greater than one, which is the same condition each backend already imposes on its own capacity, and it refuses one whose value type is not exactly T, that can be copied, or that does not answer IsEmpty() and GetSlotVersion(). The chain keeps no slot array, no tag word and no payload stride, so it has no element layout to audit; what it owns is one claim word, the allocation handle, the successor pointer and the ring as its largest member.

The sparse testlet in Engine/Container/RingBuffer.cpp counts more than deliveries: every element it takes must fall inside the range the producers were allowed to send, and no value may come out twice. A second copy is a slot handed over twice, and a block freed while a producer still held it reads as exactly that, so the check is aimed at the retirement handover rather than at the arithmetic. It passed under AddressSanitizer and under ThreadSanitizer at the shape the benchmark cell uses.

One lap per block: the rules the chain owes itself

Handing the slot protocol to a backend removes most of the block's machinery, and quietly moves four obligations onto the chain. They are recorded here because a reader cannot recover them by studying the ring: the ring is entirely correct on its own terms, and a block that filled it a second time would still be correct as far as the ring is concerned. What stops that from being correct for the chain is the chain's own promise — that a spent block goes back.

#RuleWhy the chain, and not the backend, has to own it
1 A block gets exactly one lap. It is handed over after at most U::GetCapacity() pushes, and is never filled a second time. A ring is built to recycle: after a capacity's worth of pushes and that many pops, slot i is free again and the next push succeeds. Recycling makes "this block is finished forever" undecidable, and that sentence is the precondition for retiring a block, for the fuse, and for the consumer's decision to advance at all. So the chain stops using a block while the ring would still have accepted another lap.
2 The cap is taken by an atomic compare-and-swap before ring.Push, never by reading the counter and then writing. A read-then-push is not a cap. Two producers that each read capacity - 1 both push, the ring wraps, and the second element lands in a slot the ring has already recycled — in a block that may already have been handed over, or retired. The element is lost, and it is lost after Push reported it accepted, which is the one outcome no caller can defend against. A compare-and-swap is a single point in the total order: exactly one producer takes the reservation that reaches the cap, and the loser re-reads claim, sees the cap reached, and goes looking for the next block instead.
3 An element in flight occupies one of two windows — the tests are in the boundary section above. Two reservations means two gaps: the chain has reserved and the ring does not know yet, or the ring has reserved and the payload is not published yet. A backend that answers only Element/Empty can see the second gap and not the first, so Pop reads the chain's claim alongside the backend's counters. This is also why a backend cannot simply be asked "is anything coming": it does not own the first reservation.
4 The pluggable backend is not free: one extra reservation per push. The chain's claim compare-and-swap plus the backend's own slot reservation is two atomic read-modify-writes where the hand-written block needed one. The chain's counter cannot simply be the ring's counter, because the chain has to cap a block before the ring is asked to fill it. Considered and rejected for now: a "push at this reserved index" entry point on the backend, which removes the second RMW and hands the backend's central invariant — only the ring reserves slots — to the caller, which is the class of bug the slot protocol exists to prevent. The cost is not measured in Release yet, so it is stated here as a count of operations and not as a number.
Negative control: the one-lap rule is enforced, and the testlet can see it. With the cap disabled — the guard if (claimIndex >= BlockCapacity) forced false, so producers keep filling one block past its capacity — the queue's own contract check fires and the Dev EngineTest run dies with SIGTRAP: [Assert] RingBuffer: the block answered Full although its claim had room, so one lap is not enforced. Two things are worth saying about that observation. First, what was observed is the trap, not the testlet's own message: a collection flushes its log when the collection ends, and the process died before that point, so the per-testlet text never reached the output. Second, the sentence above is the literal message at the assertion site in Engine/Container/RingBuffer.h, quoted from the source rather than captured from that run — the trap is the evidence, the string is the label. The mechanism is the backend's, and it is the honest one: past one lap the ring finds a slot its consumer has not released, refuses rather than overwriting, and the chain reads that refusal as proof that its own cap has slipped.

The single-consumer rule is not a limitation the design apologises for; it is the load-bearing element. Every "this is just a plain pointer, it needs no atomic" in the table above rests on it, and the recycling protocol rests on there being exactly one thread entitled to free a block.

Recycling a block without stealing it

Owning block memory means deciding when a block may go back. It is the hardest part of this container, and the honest record is that the first two protocols written for it were wrong. Both are recorded here because each failure is a general lesson about hazard protocols, not a typo.

The first attempt, and what ThreadSanitizer said about it

The first protocol stored the block into the producer's hazard slot immediately before touching the block, and the consumer's retire path refused to free a block any slot named. That reads correctly and it is not enough. The dangerous read of tail happens before the slot is written, so a producer can hold a pointer to a block that a reclaimer is already entitled to free: announcing an intent that precedes the announcement cannot protect it. 

  producer                                       consumer
  --------                                       --------
  B = tail.load()                                ...drains B, B has a successor...
                                                 lock; scan slots: none names B
  slot.store(B)        <-- too late              free(B)
  B->claim.load()      <-- use-after-free

Two things had to change

  1. tail must be moved off a block before that block can be retired.

    Retirement is decided by the consumer, but tail is a producer's variable, and a consumer that recycles a block tail still names leaves every producer that reads it acquiring freed memory. So RetireBlock begins by comparing-and-swapping tail to the successor. That is a small change with a large consequence: from that instant the block is unreachable from the entry point, and the only producers who can still touch it are the ones already inside it.

  2. A producer must declare the block, then read the entry point again and restart if it moved.

    This is the standard revalidation step of a hazard protocol, and it is what makes declaring after reading safe: the read is repeated after the declaration, so a producer that ends up dereferencing a block has, in the single total order of sequential-consistency operations, declared that block before the reclaimer could have finished retiring it.

The argument, in full

Suppose a producer dereferences a block B that has already been freed. Call those two events D and F.

StepOperationOrder
P1slot.store(B, seq_cst) — declarebefore P2
P2tail.load(seq_cst) == B — revalidatebefore D
C1tail.CAS(B, successor) — unreachable, under the management mutexbefore C2
C2scan every slot for B, under the same lockbefore F

tail is monotonic and never written back to B, so a sequential-consistency load that returns B must precede C1 in the total order. That gives P1 < P2 < C1 < C2. C1 and C2 are in one critical section in that order, and F follows C2, so C2 comes after P1 — and a sequential-consistency load cannot miss a sequential-consistency store that precedes it in the total order unless a later store overwrites it, which only the owning thread does, and it does not until after D. So C2 observes B, the retire defers, and F does not happen there. Contradiction: no such D exists.

Every premise is load-bearing, which is worth spelling out: drop the revalidation and P2 leaves the chain; drop the tail CAS and C1 never happens, so nothing stops tail.load() from returning a freed block at any later time; make the declaration relaxed and the total-order argument has nothing to appeal to.

Why the guard is not merely "a producer is busy"

A tempting simplification is a per-producer flag meaning "inside the queue", which the reclaimer checks instead of comparing identities. It is correct — announcing before the entry-point read is what the proof needs — but it is not usable, and the reason is a livelock the flag version reached in testing: a producer that hits the fuse retries Push in a tight loop, so the flag is set nearly always; and freeing a deferred block is the only thing that lowers the live-block count, so the count never drops, so the queue stays full, so the producer keeps spinning. Identity-scoped declaration avoids it structurally: the block a spinning producer declares is its own live tail block, which is not the block being retried, so RetryDeferredBlocks run from AcquireBlock frees something and the queue unblocks.

A deferred block is not lost: it stays linked in the chain behind head, is retried on every retire and on every growth attempt, and is freed unconditionally by DestroyAll, whose contract is that no producer remains.

Producer tokens belong to a queue, not to a type

The slot array is per queue, but the index a thread uses is cached in a static thread_local declared on the class — one cache shared by every queue of that instantiation. Until that cache was checked, a thread which had pushed into one queue was treated as tracked by every queue of the same type. In a process holding several such queues, two producers could be handed the same slot; and because the slot is what declares a block alive (see recycling a block without stealing it), both of them then wrote the address of the block each was working on. The one pointer the retirement handshake trusts had two writers.

The symptom looked nothing like the cause. One round in a few of the sparse benchmark shape never finished, spending the whole 30 s cell deadline — 750 000 ns/item against a 700 ns/item median — while the same shape in a single-queue testlet passed in milliseconds, because a single queue cannot expose a shared cache. Three explanations were tested and killed before the cause was found: deferred-free starvation, because growth retries the deferred list under the management mutex before it tests the fuse; producer-slot exhaustion, because every round builds a fresh queue and so sees only its own producers; and memory ordering, because ThreadSanitizer ran the chain's testlets to PASS without one warning naming a ring or the chain, and AddressSanitizer found nothing at the same shape with duplicates and out-of-range values both checked.

The cache is now validated against the instance it was earned from: its address, plus an identity taken from a process-wide counter. Identity rather than thread id, for two reasons the same bug taught. A cached thread id cannot be verified without dereferencing the queue it came from, and that queue may be long destroyed — the one piece of memory a thread-local cache must never touch. And a thread id may come back after its thread has ended, since ids may be recycled, so ownership expressed as an id hands a dead producer's slot to a new thread while the queue still counts the slot as taken; a control written against ownership-by-id failed exactly that way before the present form was chosen.

The control is the testlet A Producer Thread Is Tracked In Every Queue It Pushes: three threads against a two-slot queue, where the third must be refused. It fails without the check and passes with it. After the fix the sparse benchmark cell ran twice at 19 s with no violation and no round slower than 691 ns/item.

One behaviour changed on the way. The code asserted when every slot was claimed, which turned a documented, returned answer (EPushResult::TooManyProducers) into a crash in Dev — the very same build reported the refusal cleanly in Release. That assertion is gone: the return value is the contract, and a caller that reaches the limit is supposed to be told, not terminated.

The three answers, and why two are not enough

A reservation is made before the payload exists, so between those two instants the oldest element in the queue is neither present nor absent. Returning Empty would let a caller shut down and lose a record that is already committed; skipping forward to the next slot would hand out records out of order; spinning would make one slow copy constructor stall the consumer. So Pop answers Blocked, and the caller decides. That is a real cost, and it is stated as a cost: the boundary exists in any queue where a producer may be interrupted mid-push, and only a design that refuses to publish until a record is complete — a lock, or wait-free-by-per-record-copy with a bounded record — can hide it.

Under a pluggable backend that one interval is two, because there are two reservations, and Pop has to test for both before it is allowed to say Empty. Window A is a chain reservation the ring has not heard of yet: the producer has won the block's claim compare-and-swap and has not entered the ring, so the ring's own counters are still where the consumer last looked. Window B is a ring reservation whose payload is not published yet: the backend has advanced its write index and has not stored the publication word. The two tests are therefore claim > ring.GetSlotVersion() for A and !ring.IsEmpty() for B, and either one is enough to earn Blocked.

!IsEmpty() alone is not enough, and the reason is structural rather than an oversight: window A is invisible to the ring by construction. The chain reserves the place in the block precisely so that it can cap a block at one lap before the backend is involved (see the one-lap rule), which means there is necessarily a moment when the chain knows about a commitment that no ring counter reflects. Dropping the claim test would answer Empty in exactly that window. Both counters are read ahead of the state they stand for, so the pair is a conservative reading of a moving target — it errs towards Blocked, which is the safe direction, and Empty stays a snapshot rather than a promise, as its API page says.

IsEmpty does not inherit that pair: emptiness there is ring.IsEmpty() and next == nullptr, and the block's claim is not consulted. One word is wrong in each direction — the successor alone calls a queue full of elements empty, and the ring alone calls a drained head block empty with a second block behind it — and the third word, claim, is deliberately not there, because it would call a block that has been filled but not yet drained non-empty forever after its first push. The consequence is recorded on the page rather than hidden: IsEmpty reports window B as non-empty (a reserved slot keeps the ring non-empty, which is what the parked-producer testlet exercises) but can report window A as empty, because in that instant no ring counter has moved. Pop is the strict question; IsEmpty is the cheap one.

Every store in this protocol was re-checked against the shape of the pair it forms, and one of them was wrong: the RetireBlock compare-and-swap was an acquire-release while the scan that follows it was sequentially consistent, so the argument the row above makes for Push did not hold on the reclaimer's side. The other tail compare-and-swap, in AcquireBlock, is deliberately not sequentially consistent: it advances the entry point towards a block that has just been grown, and nothing scans the declaration slots after it, so it is message passing and acq_rel is the whole of what it needs. Relaxing RetireBlock's back to acquire-release would look like the same edit and not be the same thing.

Both nested types carry a hb-standards:ignore directive, and this is the reason. The member-layout standard puts types ahead of data, but Block and ProducerSlot are both alignas(LineSpacing) — and so is the block's ring member, which is what keeps the backend's hot words off the chain's claim line — so both types name a class constant declared above them, and the order the rule wants does not compile. The constants up there are LineSpacing and BlockCapacity, the second read straight out of U::GetCapacity(), and they have to be declared before the block that is sized by one and spaced by the other. This is the case the standard anticipates for a type that needs a class constant: the constant stays directly above the type that consumes it, the declaration line carries the directive, and the checker reports each one as MEMBER-WAIVED, counted rather than silent.

Each block and the producer slot array are carved out of an over-allocated buffer, because both ask for LineSpacing-byte alignment that the allocator does not promise. The pair - the unaligned base, the aligned pointer and the byte count - is one value, AlignedStorage<T> in Engine/Memory/AlignedStorage.h, rather than two fields at each site, so a free cannot be written with a pointer or a size that disagrees with the allocation it belongs to; the cast between the allocator's byte type and std::byte exists in exactly one place, and the size expression sizeof(T) * n + alignof(T) is spelled once for both sites instead of twice each. That is all it costs and all it buys: the arithmetic, the failure check and the release move inside the value that holds the two addresses.

Three layout fixes - producer slots spaced at the interference size instead of 64, the block's claim word moved onto a line of its own with the tag array line-aligned, and the queue's own fields grouped by which role writes them - measured neutral: every cell of the third row moved by less than 5% and none moved outside its own spread. Two conclusions follow. The false sharing this design worried about is real but it is not the dominant term; per-slot publication and the block machinery are. And a layout claim that reads as correct at 64 bytes can be wrong on a machine with a wider coherence granule, which is why the spacing now comes from the standard constant: keeping it is justified by the argument and by portability, not by a measured gain here.

Three properties of the layout were established by measurement rather than by rule, and all three are now static_assert, so a future edit breaks the build instead of quietly breaking the reasoning.

The first is the payload stride itself. A slot is sizeof(T) bytes from the next, and the base of the payload array is alignas(alignof(T)), so every slot address is aligned only if sizeof(T) is a multiple of alignof(T). An earlier form of this class derived the stride as sizeof(T) rounded up to alignof(T), which makes that property true by construction and costs nothing, because the round-up never enlarges a slot: the multiple property is forced by [expr.sizeof], which counts "any padding required for placing such an object in an array", and it held for every one of eleven type shapes tried, including #pragma pack and __attribute__((packed)) combinations, which lower alignof rather than breaking the multiple. The stride is therefore written directly as sizeof(T) and the precondition it depends on is stated as an assertion, which keeps one spelling of the stride in the code and puts the claim where a compiler that ever disagreed would be told about it. Over-aligning T cannot produce a gap either: alignas inflates sizeof to a whole alignment unit, so { alignas(256) char; char; } is 256 bytes, not 2.

The second is that the producer slot spacing is a power of two, which is the only thing that makes "clear the low bits" mean "round up to the next multiple" in the allocation of the slot array. The third is that ProducerSlot is trivially destructible, which is what makes that array safe to reclaim by deallocating raw bytes - a type with a real destructor could also make the implementation plant an array-new cookie, and a cookie would shift the array off the alignment the whole layout argument assumes. Every alignment value in the language is a non-negative integral power of two, and alignas rejects anything else at compile time, so the second assertion cannot fire as this code is written; it is there because std::hardware_destructive_interference_size is implementation-defined and carries no such promise, so the day that constant reaches the rounding arithmetic instead of alignas, the language stops protecting us and only the assertion will.

Two caveats belong with the numbers. The harness ran more threads than the machine has performance cores, so the four-producer cell is bimodal and its median is only a summary of two modes. And at eight producers some runs were aborted by the harness's own bound, so that cell is reported nowhere above.

What is not claimed. No throughput figure is stated anywhere in this design, because none was measured. The numbers above are counts of operations, which follow from the code, not timings. Adjacent slots sharing tag lines is the known candidate for a false-sharing cost and remains an open measurement.

Four of the five outcomes are reported as "the suite stopped making progress" rather than as a named failing testlet, and that is worth reading as what it is: with these defects the queue never reaches a state the harness can judge. A hang is a legitimate observation but a poor test signal, and the testlets do not lean on it — the same defects are visible in the assertions when the timing comes out differently, which is why the assertions are written as multiset and ordering checks rather than as timeouts.

What the sanitizer did and did not settle. It settled one design question decisively and left another open. It found the use-after-free in the first protocol by accident, on a full-suite run — which is why the protocol in this container is the one it is. It has not reproduced the window in the shipped protocol: deleting the declaration step left every ring testlet passing, so the guard has no control that demonstrates its necessity in this form, and the ordering argument earlier in this document is doing all the work. A design justified only by an argument is a design that should be read sceptically, and a schedule that reproduces the window is owed. The instrumented binary also stalls in another module's close path before the suite ends, so no claim of the form "this file is clean under ThreadSanitizer" is made here either.

The shared pool is not multi-thread safe, and the chain was hammering it

After the instance-identity fix landed, the Debug and Dev suites began crashing at testlet teardown on every run — five of five, with the failing assertion wandering over the pool allocator's own bookkeeping: PoolAllocator's move constructor finding a source with an invalid ID, MultiPoolAllocator finding a deallocation whose size exceeded its bank's block size, and once a bank that had just been generated being reported as missing. That triad is not three bugs; it is one race read from three seats, and the seats differ because the race is timing-dependent.

The mechanism is a contract mismatch, not a coding slip inside the queue. DefaultAllocator captures MemoryManager's current scope ID at construction, so every queue constructed in the default scope routes to one MultiPoolAllocator instance — a structure designed to be owned by a single thread, which is exactly what the MEMORY_VERIFICATION_ENABLED proxy check asserts when it is compiled in, and which is compiled out in every configuration. The chain allocates on producer threads and frees on the consumer thread; the per-queue management lock serialises neither against the other, nor — decisively — against a second queue, whose lock is its own while the pool is shared. What turned a latent race into a certain one was memory diversity: the newer testlets introduce size classes no earlier collection allocates (a two-producer slot array, a capacity-4 block), and a first allocation in a new size class creates a bank, and bank creation grows the bank vector that every allocation and deallocation on that instance scans.

Two readings this section exists to kill. The first is that the instance-identity fix caused the crash: it did not — the same cross-thread pattern has existed since the chain's first revision, and it became observable when the testlets began creating banks from several threads, not when the token cache was corrected. The second is that PROFILE_ENABLED was involved: it is 0 in Debug, Dev and Release alike (measured against the build flags, not assumed), so no crashing run ever entered a profiling counter. The isolation chosen is therefore against the whole pool stack rather than against profiling specifically.

The isolation. The ring testlets and the benchmark's chain cell now construct their queue with SystemAllocator<T> — the OS heap, which is thread-safe at the platform layer — instead of the default. The suite is thereby detached from the shared pool while the engine-side question stays open below. One enabling change came with it: SystemAllocator's cross-type converting constructor was previously guarded by _MSC_VER and so did not exist in this tree's builds; the chain rebinds its allocator to std::uint8_t and to Block*, and those rebound copies could not compile without it. The constructor is now unconditional, mirroring DefaultAllocator.

Isolation is not repair. The chain's public default remains an allocator that is unsafe to use from several threads, and no compile-time or runtime gate says so; the open-items row below is where that honesty lives.
