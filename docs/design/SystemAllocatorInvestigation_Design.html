System allocator investigation mode design

The SystemAllocator reference pages own what a caller depends on; this document owns the investigation mode behind them — the page-rounded allocation shape, the single guard page, the placement selector and the probe. Every claim was read from Engine/Memory/SystemAllocator.h.

The investigation mode and its three switches

MEMORY_INVESTIGATION_ENABLED replaces malloc/free with page-granular virtual blocks: every allocation is its own OS::VirtualAlloc mapping, rounded to whole pages, and every release returns it — or protects it. The mode is the engine's Electric Fence: commercial speed and density are traded away so that memory defects are caught by the hardware rather than by luck. Two switches shape what it watches:

  • MEMORY_BUFFER_UNDERRUN_CHECK_ENABLED — the placement selector: where inside the guarded region the caller's bytes sit.
  • MEMORY_DANGLING_POINTER_CHECK_ENABLED — whether release ProtectMemorys the mapping in place (every later access faults) or VirtualFrees it back to the OS.

All three are hardwired to 0 in Engine/Config/BuildConfig.h, and the feature-macro proof compiles the guarded paths so that a dormant mode cannot rot — a block nobody has ever compiled is where defects hid before, which is the lesson the profiling macros taught first.

One guard page, on the watched side

The mapping is pageCount + 1 pages, and exactly one of them is protected PROT_NONE/PAGE_NOACCESS at allocation time — and its position follows the placement selector: front placement protects the first page, end placement protects the last. The caller's bytes sit on the other side of the guard, so a one-byte crossing of the watched boundary faults — deterministically, because the fault's cause is the guard page's own protection bit and not the accident of what the OS happened to map next door.

Owner decision, 2026-10-25: one guard, not two. A two-guard variant was considered and rejected: the second guard would have made the unwatched side's large crossings fault too (and given page-multiple allocations byte resolution on both sides for free), but the practical value was judged low against the extra page. The accepted consequence is stated plainly: on the unwatched side, a crossing that leaves the mapping lands in whatever the OS mapped next door — on Linux that is usually a neighbouring allocation, so the corruption is silent. The mode's contract is the watched side only.

The placement selector chooses the check, exclusively

Switch stateLayoutWatched boundaryUnwatched side
MEMORY_BUFFER_UNDERRUN_CHECK_ENABLED 1 [GUARD][data pages], ptr = rawPtr + pageSize under-run at 1-byte resolution (ptr[-1] is the guard) over-run is not checked: sub-page slack absorbs it silently, a crossing leaves the mapping into the OS neighbour
MEMORY_BUFFER_UNDERRUN_CHECK_ENABLED 0 [data pages][GUARD], ptr = rawPtr + pageCount × pageSize − nBytes over-run at 1-byte resolution (ptr[nBytes] is the guard) under-run is not checked: the same silence, mirrored

The two checks are mutually exclusive by construction: there is one switch, and its two states are the two checks — a single bit cannot assert both. This is deliberate and recorded as the switch's contract: the mode hunts one boundary per run, chosen at build time, and the page-level slack on the unwatched side is silent in every configuration. The end placement's recovery arithmetic (rawPtr = ptr + nBytes − pageCount × pageSize) is the inverse of its placement, exactly as the front placement's rawPtr = ptr − pageSize is of its own.

The probe: page protection is the freed state

In MEMORY_DANGLING_POINTER_CHECK_ENABLED mode a release does not return the mapping to the OS — it protects the whole of it, guards included. The address therefore never comes back into circulation, and page protection is the state that says "freed". That makes the double-release case detectable with no bookkeeping at all: the next DeallocateBytes of the same pointer begins by reading one byte through it — the probe. A live allocation answers silently; a freed one faults at the probing call, whose stack names the second release. The bitmap alternative needed a second source of truth to keep in step with the free list; here the protection bit is the truth. What the probe still does not catch is a release of an address that was freed and re-allocated to someone else — the fault lands on whoever owns the mapping now, which is the stale-pointer class, and closing it needs generations, not protection.

What the mode costs, and why it stays dormant

CostFigure
Memory per allocation+1 guard page (4 KB at 4 KB pages) — 100-byte objects occupy 8 KB
Memory on release (dangling mode)the mapping is never returned — RSS grows monotonically for the life of the process
Speedone mmap and one mprotect call per allocation; one probe read and one mprotect per release
Detection resolutionone byte on the watched side only; the unwatched side is unchecked by the owner decision above

The price is only sane where the engine is hunting a defect, which is why every switch ships 0 and the test-suite fault-injection tests (SystemAllocator.cpp, behind MEMORY_INVESTIGATOR_TEST_ENABLED) are manual: they expect the signal, and an automated run that expects a crash cannot be green.
